Demographic economics examines how family decisions, population dynamics, and social norms shape economic and social outcomes across generations. Fertility behavior, marriage, migration, gender norms, and household structure influence education, health, labor market participation, and long-term well-being. Understanding how these demographic processes interact with inequality and social institutions is important for designing policies that improve human welfare and reduce vulnerability.

This research agenda studies how household behavior, gender preferences, and demographic transitions affect economic and social outcomes in low- and middle-income countries. Current projects examine topics such as son preference, fertility behavior, women’s health, domestic violence, and intergenerational household dynamics, with a focus on how social norms and demographic pressures shape well-being and inequality.

Current Projects

Project 1: Beyond Fertility: Son Preference and Women’s Health in Nepal

Although stated preferences for sons have declined in many societies, son preference continues to influence family decisions and women’s well-being. This project examines how pressure to have sons affects fertility behavior and maternal health outcomes among women in Nepal. Using nationally representative survey data, the research explores how families whose first child is a daughter differ in fertility patterns, pregnancy behavior, contraceptive use, and health outcomes.

Project 2: Push and Pull Factors for (Early) Marriage, Bangladesh Integrated Household Survey Questionnaire (BIHS)

Early marriage remains an important social and economic issue in many developing countries, affecting women’s education, health, and long-term economic opportunities. This project examines the social, economic, and household factors associated with marriage timing in Bangladesh using data from the Bangladesh Integrated Household Survey (BIHS).

UP Researchers: Akito Kameiwith Dr. Mary Arends-Kuenning, Dr. Abigail Stocker, and Farhana Kabir

Son Preference and Its Impact on Family Size and Domestic Violence Against Mothers in South Asia

In many parts of South Asia, strong preferences for sons continue to shape family decisions, fertility behavior, and women’s well-being. This project examines how the sex composition of children influences family size and the risk of intimate partner violence (IPV) against mothers in India and Nepal.

Using nationally representative household survey data, the research explores whether families without sons experience greater social and economic pressures and how these pressures affect women’s lives. The project also investigates how decisions to continue childbearing contribute to household stress and violence. The findings highlight the broader social consequences of gender preference and contribute to discussions on gender inequality, reproductive behavior, and women’s health in developing countries.
