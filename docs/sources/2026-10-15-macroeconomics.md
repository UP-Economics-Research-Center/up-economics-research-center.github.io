Macroeconomics studies how economies grow, respond to crises, and create conditions for long-term prosperity. Government policies, financial systems, international trade, and political institutions all shape economic stability, productivity, and development opportunities. Understanding how economies react to shocks such as debt crises, inflation, or financial instability is important for designing policies that promote sustainable growth and protect household welfare.

This research agenda examines how macroeconomic institutions, sovereign risk, trade integration, and public policy influence economic development and long-term growth. Current projects explore topics such as sovereign default, productivity losses during financial crises, trade agreements, monetary institutions, and the broader relationship between macroeconomic stability and economic performance.

Current Project

Project 1: Inflation Expectations and the Phillips Curve in Mexico

This project examines the credibility of inflation targeting in Mexico and how inflation expectations have evolved since the adoption of the inflation-targeting regime. Despite a long-standing inflation target of 3 percent, realized inflation has frequently deviated from this benchmark. The study investigates whether these deviations have affected the extent to which households and markets view the target as credible. By analyzing the relationship between inflation dynamics and expectations over time, the project aims to provide new evidence on the effectiveness of monetary policy and the anchoring of inflation expectations in Mexico.UP Researcher: Juan Alvaro D. Monetary Policy Under  Time-Varying Uncertainty Aversion

How should central banks conduct monetary policy when uncertainty about the economy changes over time? This study develops a framework in which an inflation-targeting central bank faces varying degrees of uncertainty regarding the structure of the economy and adjusts its policy accordingly. Using a robust control approach combined with a Markov-switching framework, the analysis shows that policymakers respond more aggressively with interest rate adjustments when uncertainty aversion is higher or when the likelihood of future uncertainty increases. The results also indicate that greater uncertainty reduces policymakers’ welfare, highlighting the challenges central banks face when designing monetary policy in evolving and uncertain economic environments.

UP Researcher: Arnulfo Rodríguez Hernández With Fidel Gonzalez (Sam Houston State University) Computational Economics, 2011

The Productivity Cost of Sovereign Default: Evidence from the European Debt Crisis

What are the economic consequences when governments default on their sovereign debt? This study examines how sovereign default affects productivity and economic growth during the European debt crisis using a continuous-time macroeconomic model calibrated with data from European countries between 2009 and 2012.

The findings show that sovereign default can generate substantial productivity losses, with total factor productivity (TFP) declining between 3.7% and 5.9% following default episodes. The model helps explain the sharp declines in GDP growth observed during debt crises and suggests that fiscal stimulus policies may have limited effects in such periods. The study highlights how financial instability and sovereign risk can create long-lasting macroeconomic costs for economic development and recovery.

UP Researcher: Esteban Colla‐De‐Robertis With Jorge Alonso-Ortiz and José-María Da-Rocha Economic Theory, 2017
