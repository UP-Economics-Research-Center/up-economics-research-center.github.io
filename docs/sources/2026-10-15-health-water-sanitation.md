Health is a fundamental component of human well-being and economic development, yet millions of people around the world continue to face preventable health risks linked to poverty, inadequate infrastructure, and limited access to essential services. Unsafe water, inadequate sanitation, poor hygiene, and malnutrition remain major contributors to disease, mortality, and long-term deficits in human capital, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, while obesity and diet-related chronic diseases have become growing public health challenges in advanced economies.

This research agenda in health economics examines how environmental conditions, household behavior, and public health interventions influence health outcomes and economic well-being. Current research is organized around two main strands: Water and Sanitation, which focuses on water treatment interventions and household responses to water quality risks, and Nutritional Health, which examines nutrition, food environments, and their implications for health, productivity, and human development.

Current Projects

Project 1: Water Treatment, E. coli in Drinking Water, and Diarrhea: Causal Inference Using Double/Debiased Machine Learning

Household decisions to treat drinking water are influenced by a wide range of socioeconomic and environmental factors, making causal inference difficult in observational settings. This study applies Double/Debiased Machine Learning (DDML) to estimate the causal effects of household water treatment on E. coli contamination and child diarrhea across 25 countries in Sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America, and Asia.

UP Researcher: Juan Alvaro Diaz Raimond Kedilhac, Akito Kamei

Project 2: Spatial Inequality in Access to Kidney Transplantation in Mexico

How much does where you live determine your chances of receiving a kidney transplant in Mexico? This project explores how geography shapes access to transplantation, focusing on differences in patients’ likelihood of receiving a transplant and how long they must wait. By combining patient, hospital, and regional information, it highlights where access barriers are greatest and where transplant capacity may be most unevenly distributed.UP Researchers: María Isabel Espín, Román Rodríguez Aguilar (Business School) with Dr. Enrique Minor Campa (Development Studies, Centro de Investigación y Docencia Económicas, CIDE)

Project 3: Intergenerational Transmission of Obesity in Mexico

Obesity has increased rapidly in many developing countries, creating important challenges for public health and long-term well-being. This project examines how obesity is transmitted across generations in Mexico and explores whether these patterns differ between mothers, fathers, sons, and daughters. Using nationally representative health survey data, the research studies the relationship between parental obesity and adolescent obesity in the context of Mexico’s ongoing nutritional transition. UP Researchers: Andrea Corte Aréchiga, Akito Kamei

In-line chlorination for drinking water in rural Odisha, India: A pilot randomized controlled trialA randomized controlled trial in rural Odisha, India examined the effectiveness of in-line chlorination systems integrated into piped drinking water networks. The intervention significantly reduced E. coli contamination and antibiotic-resistant bacteria in drinking water while decreasing the household time burden of water treatment—particularly for women—highlighting the potential of scalable water treatment technologies to improve public health.UP Researcher: Akito Kameiwith Jeremy Lowe, Vaishnavi Prathap, Sidhartha Giri, Krushna Sahoo, Michael Kremer, Elisa Maffioli, and Amy Pickering. PLOS Water, 2026.

Water treatment and E. coli in drinking water: Household responses to (invisible) water quality risksThis study investigates how often household water sources are contaminated with E. coli, a key indicator of fecal contamination, and whether families respond by treating their water. Using nationally representative data from nearly 60,000 households across 25 low- and middle-income countries, the study finds that water contamination is common, yet most households do not treat their drinking water. Even when E. coli is present, treatment rates increase only slightly. Strikingly, many households that report treating their water still have contaminated stored water, suggesting that treatment practices may be inconsistent or ineffective.UP Researcher: Akito Kameiwith Bhowmik Sujey Soori .PLOS One, 2026.
