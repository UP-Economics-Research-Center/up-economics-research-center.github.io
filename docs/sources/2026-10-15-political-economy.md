Political and economic institutions play a central role in shaping economic development, public policy, and long-term social outcomes. Decisions related to governance, property rights, trade integration, monetary policy, and regulation influence how societies allocate resources, respond to crises, and create opportunities for growth and innovation. Differences in institutional quality and incentive structures can lead to large disparities in productivity, inequality, and economic stability across countries and regions.

This research agenda examines how institutions, governance structures, and incentives affect economic development and macroeconomic outcomes. Current projects explore topics such as free trade agreements, sovereign default, monetary policy committees, land tenure systems, innovation networks, natural resource governance, and the role of local institutions in shaping economic behavior and development trajectories.

The Decline of Non-Military Montepíos in Early Mexico: A Historical, Actuarial, and Financial Approach

Why did some of Mexico’s earliest public pension institutions fail? This article examines the decline of non-military Montepíos—pension-paying institutions for civil servants in New Spain and early independent Mexico—after 1821. Rather than viewing their collapse mainly as a consequence of the political and fiscal turmoil that followed independence, the article argues that weaknesses in the pension systems’ own design were central. These Montepíos operated largely on a pay-as-you-go basis, using current contributions to finance current benefits, but they lacked mechanisms to keep contributions and future obligations in balance. Drawing on archival records and demographic evidence, the article shows that contributors often paid into the system for relatively short periods while pensions, especially survivor pensions, could continue for many years. Gender and marriage patterns further extended these payment periods. The findings show that problems commonly associated with modern pension systems—underfunding, demographic pressure, and a mismatch between contributions and benefits—were already present in early welfare institutions in Spanish America.

The Effect of a Free Trade Agreement with the United States on Member Countries’ Economic Development

Do free trade agreements (FTAs) with the United States promote long-term economic development? This study examines the impact of FTAs on countries’ per capita GDP using the synthetic control method, which constructs a counterfactual scenario to estimate what economic outcomes would have looked like without the agreement.

The findings reveal that the effects of FTAs are highly heterogeneous across countries. While agreements with the U.S. appear to have increased per capita GDP in countries such as Chile and Jordan, the results suggest that NAFTA may have negatively affected Mexico’s per capita GDP. The study also finds that countries more dependent on trade with the United States tend to experience smaller economic gains from FTAs, highlighting that trade agreements alone do not guarantee economic development.

Local Institutions in Peru and the Presence of Fast-Food Outlets

Local governments and institutions can shape economic development by influencing business investment, infrastructure, and the business environment. This study examines how the quality of local institutions affects the presence and expansion of fast-food outlets across urban districts in Peru. Using municipal-level data, the research explores how factors such as urban management capacity, administrative systems, and local investment planning are associated with business activity and market entry. The findings suggest that stronger local institutions and better infrastructure are linked to greater private sector investment and economic activity. The study highlights the important role of local governance in shaping regional economic development and attracting investment.
